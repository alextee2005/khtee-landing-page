## PART 1 — THE CUSTOMER AVATAR

### 1.1 Primary avatar

**Rachel Tan Mei Xin, 33.** Malaysian-Chinese. Lives in Petaling Jaya, works in Kuala Lumpur.
Pronouns: she/her.

**Occupation**
- Assistant Vice President, Finance Business Partner, at a regional bank's group office. Nine years in: Big Four audit → corporate finance → banking.

- Passed over once already. The feedback was: *"You're very strong technically. We just need more executive presence from you."* Nobody defined "executive presence." Nobody ever does.

- She is the family CFO — the one everyone calls about insurance, about the hospital bill, about whether to sell the Ipoh house. Sandwich generation, both sides pressing.
- Big family decisions get discussed with Kenneth first. Any purchase over ~RM 3,000 gets discussed with Kenneth. This matters enormously for the close.

**Recreation**
- Saturday brunch and mall time with Ezra. Sunday lunch at her in-laws'.
- A gym membership she uses maybe five times a month. Badminton when someone organises it.
- Netflix after Ezra sleeps. K-dramas, true crime.
- LinkedIn at 11:40pm in bed — scrolling colleagues' promotion posts, feeling something she wouldn't say out loud.
- Podcasts and YouTube on the commute: business interviews, productivity, local finance channels.
- A graveyard of unfinished online courses. Two Coursera certificates, one Udemy Excel course, an AI course she bought during a launch and watched 20% of. She feels guilty about this, and it is her single biggest hidden objection.
- Travels 1–2 times a year. Bangkok, Bali, Da Nang. Japan is on the list "when things settle down."
- Active in three WhatsApp groups that actually matter: uni friends, ex-Big Four colleagues, family.

**Dreams**
- **Stated dream:** Director or Head of by 38. A seat at the table where decisions are actually made.
- **Real dream underneath:** to walk into a room full of senior men and not feel her voice go thin. To be the person other people quote when she is not there.
- Buy her parents a proper house, or at least clear the medical costs before they become a crisis. This is not a nice-to-have. It is the whole reason she works.
- Financial optionality: enough that a bad boss is an inconvenience, not a catastrophe.
- Eventually: a board seat, or advisory work, or something of her own. She is embarrassed to say this out loud because she has no idea how anyone gets there.
- For Ezra to grow up knowing his mother was somebody.

**Fears (ranked by how often they wake her at 3am)**
1. Being made redundant at 41 with a mortgage and two dependent parents.
2. Being the reliable one forever. Competent, essential, invisible, unpromoted.
3. AI eating the exact part of her job she was proudest of — and a 27-year-old who is fluent in it eating the rest.
4. Her parents needing money she does not have.
5. Being found out. She has never fully believed she deserves the job she already has.
6. Saying something clumsy in English in a townhall in front of the regional head.

**Cultural characteristics that drive the buying decision**
- **Collectivist orientation.** Her ambition is framed as duty, not self-actualisation. "I want to be rich" is uncomfortable. "I want to take care of my parents" is not. *Copy must let her want more by making it about them.*
- **High power distance.** She was raised to defer. Challenging a senior feels physically wrong. This is why "just speak up more" advice fails her. (Hofstede scores Malaysia extremely high on Power Distance — see sources.)
- **Face.** Failing in public is much worse than failing. She will not join anything that risks visible humiliation — this rules out "hot seat" formats unless you explicitly de-risk them.
- **The self-promotion bind.** She has been taught that self-promotion is arrogance, *and* she is being punished for not self-promoting. She is stuck between two rules. **Whoever resolves this bind for her wins the sale.** This is the single most important emotional lever in the whole avatar.
- **Scam fatigue.** Her feeds are full of crypto boys, MLM, "6-figure coach" ads, Telegram investment scams. She has a friend who lost money. Her default assumption about any paid program is that it is a scam. Credentials, verifiable institutions and specificity are not "nice trust signals" here — they are the price of entry.
- **Hard-work meritocracy belief, now cracking.** She still half-believes effort should be enough, and she is quietly angry that it isn't. Give her permission to be angry at the *system*, not at herself.

**Buying behaviour**
- Awareness stage: **problem-aware, edging into solution-aware** (Eugene Schwartz's scale). She knows something is wrong and roughly what it's called ("visibility", "executive presence"). She does not know your program exists.
- Market sophistication: **stage 3–4**. She has seen every "get promoted" claim. Claims alone will bounce. She needs a **named mechanism** that explains *why* she has been stuck.
- Research depth: high. She will read the entire page, open your LinkedIn in another tab, check whether the CFA charter is real, and ask one friend.
- Ambiguity aversion: high. Vague deliverables kill the sale. She wants to know exactly how many sessions, what days, what time, on what platform.
- Price sensitivity: moderate, but **cash-flow sensitive**. A 3-part payment plan can double conversion on the same total price for this avatar.
- Decision style: decides emotionally at night, then builds an ROI justification in the morning to present to Kenneth. **You must write the justification for her.**
- Time to purchase: 3–21 days. She will not buy on first read unless there is a real deadline.

### SECTION A — Qualifying eyebrow

> **For Southeast Asian professionals with 8+ years' experience who are tired of being the best-kept secret in their own company.**

**Principles:** Message-to-market match (Dan Kennedy); self-selection / qualification increases perceived exclusivity and reduces reactance (Brehm's reactance theory, 1966); "unity" — the shared in-group identity (Cialdini's 7th principle, *Influence: New and Expanded*, 2021).

### SECTION B — Headline

> **# Five people are in a room right now deciding your next ten years. You are not in that room.**

**Principles:** Enter the conversation already happening in the prospect's mind (Robert Collier, *The Robert Collier Letter Book*, 1931). Curiosity + self-interest, the two strongest headline drivers in split tests (John Caples, *Tested Advertising Methods*). Loss framing — a threat to something she already has beats a promise of gain (Kahneman & Tversky, prospect theory, 1979).

### SECTION C — Sub-headline

> **You will never get into that room. But you can change what is said in it. This is the system for doing that — built by a CFA charterholder who sits in those rooms every week.**

**Principles:** Concession → credibility (the "damaging admission"; also the pratfall effect, Aronson, Willerman & Floyd, 1966). Authority (Cialdini). Mechanism promise for a sophisticated market (Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising*, 1966).

### SECTION D — The lead (story)

> Let me guess how your last promotion cycle went.
>
> You worked. Properly worked. You stayed back. You fixed the model at 1am. You covered for the teammate who didn't. Your numbers were clean.
>
> Your boss said, "I don't know what we'd do without you."
>
> Then the announcement came. It wasn't you.
>
> It was the one who talks more than they work. The one who is always in the boss's room. The one who somehow knows the regional head's wife's name.
>
> And when you asked why, you got the words. You remember them exactly:
>
> *"You're very strong technically. We just need to see more presence."*
> *"Let's revisit next cycle."*
> *"You need more visibility."*
>
> Nobody told you what any of that means. Nobody ever does.

**Principles:** Specificity creates belief (Claude Hopkins, *Scientific Advertising*, 1923). Narrative transportation lowers counter-arguing (Green & Brock, 2000). "Justify their failures" and "confirm their suspicions" — 2 of the 5 drivers in Blair Warren's One-Sentence Persuasion. The slippery slide: every sentence exists to pull you into the next (Joseph Sugarman, *The Adweek Copywriting Handbook*).

### SECTION E — Agitation and the enemy

> Here is what nobody in this part of the world says out loud, because it sounds rude.
>
> Your parents told you: study hard, work hard, keep your head down, and you will be rewarded.
>
> They were not lying to you. They were describing a world that no longer exists.
>
> That advice worked when there were more chairs than people. Today there are forty qualified people for every chair. AI is quietly absorbing the parts of your job you were proudest of. And the person who decides who gets the chair has never once watched you work.
>
> They have only heard *about* you. Second-hand. In a room. For about ninety seconds.
>
> Those ninety seconds are your career.

**Principles:** Externalise the enemy — attack the outdated rule, never the reader (Blair Warren, "throw rocks at their enemies"). Loss aversion + availability of a vivid scenario (Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, 2011). Problem→agitate→solve (classic direct-response structure, popularised by Dan Kennedy).

### SECTION F — Accusation audit

> You are probably thinking one of three things.
>
> **"This is another course by another guru."** Fair. Most of them have never managed a budget, never sat on a board, never had to give a CEO bad news. You should be suspicious. I would be.
>
> **"I don't have the time."** Also fair. You have a job, a toddler, and parents who call.
>
> **"Isn't promoting yourself a bit... arrogant?"** This is the real one. And it is the most Asian objection there is.

**Principles:** The accusation audit — say their objection before they do, and it loses its charge (Chris Voss, *Never Split the Difference*, 2016). Two-sided argument increases persuasion with educated audiences (Hovland, Lumsdaine & Sheffield, 1949).

### SECTION G — Resolving the bind

> We were raised to believe that talking about your work is showing off. That wanting to be seen is cheap.
>
> I am not going to tell you that instinct is wrong. I was raised with it too.
>
> I am going to tell you it is expensive.
>
> Being quiet does not make you humble. It makes you easy to overlook — and it makes your parents wait longer.

**Principles:** Reframing rather than contradicting (motivational-interviewing style; also Cialdini's liking/similarity). Collectivist re-anchoring of ambition onto family duty — the culturally acceptable justification (Hofstede individualism/collectivism; Markus & Kitayama, 1991, interdependent self-construal).

### SECTION H — The mechanism

> Everything that has ever changed your working life was decided in a room you were not in.
>
> The promotion. A room. The high-visibility project. A room. The redundancy list. A room. The board seat. A room.
>
> You cannot get into that room. No course can change that.
>
> But your **name** can be in that room. And what is said about your name in that room can be engineered.
>
> That is the whole game. I call it **the Decision Room.**

**Principles:** Unique mechanism — mandatory at market sophistication stage 3–4 (Schwartz). Naming the mechanism makes it ownable and memorable (von Restorff / distinctiveness effect). "It's not your fault, it's the mechanism" removes shame, which unblocks buying (Bandura's self-efficacy, 1977 — belief that the outcome is achievable *by them*).

### SECTION I — The six levers

> Six things move what is said about you in that room. Not one. Six. Miss one and the rest leak away.
>
> **1. FINANCE — speak the only language every room agrees on.** Every decision ends in a number. If you can't tell your story in money, someone else tells it for you, badly.
> **2. AI — take back your evenings.** AI will take work off someone in your team. Make sure it takes yours first, so you are free to do the work that gets noticed.
> **3. COMMUNICATION — say it so it survives without you.** Your idea has to reach that room through someone else's mouth and still be recognisably yours. That is a trainable skill.
> **4. LEADERSHIP — become the person they bet on.** Rooms don't reward past performance. They bet on predicted performance. You are managing a bet.
> **5. PSYCHOLOGY — read the real rules.** Humans have run the same status software for 200,000 years: rank, coalitions, reputation, reciprocity. An organisation is that software running on office furniture. Once you see it, you stop taking it personally and start working with it.
> **6. NETWORK AND BRAND — get discussed in rooms you will never enter.** The best opportunity of your life will be handed to you by someone you have not met, in a conversation you will never hear.

**Principles:** Rule of specificity and the "reason why" (Hopkins). Chunking six disparate subjects under one organising idea beats a list of modules (Miller, 1956, chunking; Heath & Heath, *Made to Stick*, 2007 — simple, concrete). Each lever is written as a benefit, not a topic.

### SECTION J — Authority and unity

> I'm Alex Tee. CFA charterholder. Over ten years across Malaysia and Cambodia.
>
> Today I report directly to the CEO of a state-owned development bank. I own the ten-year strategy and the business plans under it. I designed the general ledger for our Temenos T24 core banking system and took it live. I built the accounting framework for a USD 100 million climate finance facility. I sit on a board as an independent director and chair its investment committee. I grew a Big Four risk practice from five people to more than forty. I have trained over 100 staff to use AI tools. I've served as president of my Toastmasters club and mentored more than ten speakers.
>
> I am telling you this for one reason: **I am in those rooms. And I was not always let in.**
>
> I started as an audit intern in Kuala Lumpur. I was the quiet one who did the work. I watched louder, less capable people get chosen, and I told myself exactly what you are telling yourself now — that it would be noticed eventually.
>
> It was not noticed.

**Principles:** Authority via verifiable, checkable, institutional credentials (Cialdini) — essential in a scam-saturated market. Unity: "I am one of you" (Cialdini, 2021). The hero's-journey "I was where you are" structure converts authority into hope without losing status.

### SECTION K — The offer

> **[PROGRAM NAME] — [X] weeks. [N] people. One cohort.**
> [Insert: number of live sessions, day/time, platform, 1-to-1 calls, WhatsApp access window, templates, recordings, alumni access, certificate.]

**Principles:** Ambiguity aversion (Ellsberg, 1961) — every unspecified detail is a reason to delay. Value stacking with individually anchored components (Hormozi, *$100M Offers*, 2021 — value = dream outcome × perceived likelihood ÷ time × effort).

### SECTION L — Price and risk reversal

> [Anchor against the cost of one missed promotion. Then price. Then 3-part payment plan. Then guarantee.]

**Principles:** Anchoring and adjustment (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). Payment-plan pain-of-paying reduction (Prelec & Loewenstein, 1998). Risk reversal shifts risk from buyer to seller and is the single highest-leverage conversion element in direct response (Jay Abraham; Dan Kennedy).

### SECTION M — Scarcity and close

> [Honest cohort cap and closing date. Two-futures close. P.S.]

**Principles:** Scarcity (Worchel, Lee & Adewole, 1975 — the cookie-jar study). Deadlines convert intention into action (implementation intentions, Gollwitzer, 1999). The P.S. is the second-most-read element of a long sales letter after the headline (direct-mail testing lore; see caveat in sources).

## PART 3 — SELF-REVIEW OF VERSION 1

Scored against the criteria that actually predict conversion. Honest marks.

| # | Criterion | Score | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Message-to-market match | 9/10 | Strong. The bind (raised not to self-promote, punished for not self-promoting) is the right nerve. |
| 2 | Headline strength | 8/10 | Good hook, but "next ten years" is abstract. No named audience in the headline itself. |
| 3 | Unique mechanism | 8/10 | "The Decision Room" is ownable and true. But it's introduced too late — after ~500 words. |
| 4 | Proof | **2/10** | **The critical failure.** Alex's credentials are excellent, but they prove *he* is successful, not that *he can make her* successful. There is no student result anywhere. |
| 5 | Specificity of offer | **3/10** | Section K is a skeleton. This avatar has high ambiguity aversion. Vagueness here kills the sale outright. |
| 6 | Risk reversal | **2/10** | Named but not written. A guarantee that isn't spelled out is worth nothing. |
| 7 | Objection handling | 6/10 | The accusation audit is good but never closes the loop on "I never finish courses" — her #1 private objection. |
| 8 | Urgency | **2/10** | Placeholder only. Without a real deadline this avatar reads, bookmarks, and never returns. |
| 9 | Spouse/decision-unit handling | **1/10** | Completely missing. She needs Kenneth's agreement for a purchase this size. Not writing his justification is leaving money on the table. |
| 10 | Readability / plain English | 8/10 | Mostly short sentences. A few paragraphs run long. "Externalise", "engineered" are slightly above the target reading level. |
| 11 | Cultural fit | 9/10 | Face, filial duty, deference and scam fatigue are all handled. |
| 12 | Single clear call to action | 5/10 | No CTA appears until the very end. On mobile, most readers never get there. |

**The five things that must change:**

1. **Proof is missing.** Everything else is decoration until this is fixed. Add a dedicated proof block. If no student results exist yet, say so honestly and use a beta/founding-cohort frame — which is *more* credible than vague claims, not less.
2. **The offer is not concrete.** Number of sessions, dates, times, platform, exactly what she receives. Write it like a contract, not a brochure.
3. **The guarantee must be written in full,** in one sentence a lawyer would accept and a sceptic would believe.
4. **Add a "for Kenneth" block** — a short, forwardable ROI paragraph she can screenshot and send to her spouse. Almost nobody writes this. It is worth a large share of the lift for this avatar.
5. **Add the "I never finish courses" objection**, and answer it structurally (accountability, small cohort, homework reviewed by a human) rather than with encouragement.

**Two further improvements:**
6. Move the mechanism earlier and repeat the CTA at least three times down the page.
7. Cut roughly 15% of the words. Every sentence that does not create desire, belief or action should go.
